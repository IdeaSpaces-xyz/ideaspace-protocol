# The Story

## The problem it solves

Right now, everything you and your assistant work out together lives in a chat window. Next
session, it's gone. You explain your context again. And again. The thinking survives, but the
understanding doesn't.

## The idea

A **space** is a folder on your computer that holds two things: your stuff, and a short note
explaining what this place is about.

Think of a well-run workshop. There's the work itself — the projects on the bench. And there's a
whiteboard by the door: what we're building, why, what's in progress right now, what's next.
Anyone who walks in reads the whiteboard and can be useful in five minutes, without you stopping
to brief them.

The folder is the workshop. The whiteboard is the part that makes it a space.

## Why it matters

The whiteboard means your assistant arrives already knowing where things stand. You stop being
the person who has to remember and re-explain everything.

## Four things worth knowing

- **Rooms can have their own whiteboards.** A folder inside a folder can add its own notes —
  specific to that corner of the work, inheriting the rest. The deeper you go, the more specific
  it gets.
- **Writing things down is deliberate.** When you work something out together, it becomes a note
  with a title and a one-line summary — so later, you or your assistant can tell what's in it
  without opening it. Saving what you asked for happens right away; your assistant checks with
  you before anything leaves your machine.
- **Nothing is thrown away.** Every version is kept, so you can always look back at what changed
  and when.
- **It's yours, on your machine.** Ordinary files on your own computer, no account required.
  Putting it online,
  sharing it with someone, reaching it from another computer — all optional, added later if you
  want them.

## How to start

You don't design a space up front. You capture one real thing, then another, and the structure
appears from what's actually there. The instinct to plan the folders first is the wrong one.
